The logo trap

The first thing most founders do when starting a company is design a logo. It's understandable — logos are tangible, they signal arrival, they feel like proof. But building a logo before you've built a brand is like designing a flag before choosing your country.

A logo is a retrieval cue. It works by activating associations already formed in someone's mind. Nike's swoosh means something not because of its shape, but because of thirty years of associating that shape with athletic achievement, cultural relevance, and a very specific attitude. Strip away those associations and you're left with a checkmark.

What comes before the logo

Before a single pixel gets designed, a brand needs answers to three questions: What do you believe? Who are you for? And what do you promise to deliver, every single time? These aren't marketing statements. They're operating principles that should constrain every decision a company makes — including visual ones.

The right order

Build your brand in this order: positioning, then messaging, then visual identity. Positioning answers the strategic question of where you fit in the competitive landscape. Messaging translates that positioning into language that resonates with real humans. Visual identity translates both into a system that communicates nonverbally, instantly, and at scale.

When you get the order wrong, you end up with beautiful brands that mean nothing. When you get it right, you end up with brands like Stripe — whose visual identity is so precise and distinctive that it communicates sophistication and trustworthiness before a single word is read.
