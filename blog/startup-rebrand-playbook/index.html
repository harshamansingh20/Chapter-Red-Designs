Why most rebrands fail

Most startup rebrands are triggered by the wrong signal. A new investor doesn't like the logo. A competitor looks more polished. The CEO gets bored. These are aesthetic reasons for a strategic operation, and they almost always produce expensive mediocrity.

The right trigger for a rebrand is strategic misalignment — when the visual identity no longer reflects what the company actually is, who it actually serves, or where it's actually going. That's a problem worth solving. Cosmetic discomfort is not.

The three diagnostic questions

Before any rebrand begins, answer these: Does our current brand reflect who we've become, or who we were when we started? Are we winning or losing deals because of how we look — and do we have evidence for that? And if we changed nothing visually but fixed our positioning and messaging, would the problem go away?

IDENTITY
Fix the strategy first. Then fix the design.

How to execute without losing momentum

The practical danger of a rebrand is the runway it burns. Creative work expands to fill whatever time is allocated, and the transition period between brands creates confusion in the market. The solution is to treat the rebrand like a product launch: fixed scope, fixed deadline, clear launch event.

Set a go-live date before you start. Define the minimum viable scope — the assets you absolutely must have at launch versus what can follow. And communicate the change proactively to customers, not reactively. A rebrand that surprises your existing customers is a rebrand that feels erratic, even if the work is excellent.
